# IP boundaries for an independent counterfactual game

**Research date:** 2026-09-09  
**Status:** Product-planning research, not legal advice

## Scope and limits

This memo gives a U.S. copyright and trademark planning baseline, with limited U.K. and EU comparisons. It does not establish freedom to operate in every country. Copyright, trademark, passing-off, personality/publicity, patent, and unfair-competition rules are territorial and fact-specific.

The ticket does not identify the television program, episode, scene, rights holders, intended launch countries, final title, final marketing copy, or final visual design. Those omissions prevent source-specific rights clearance and a legal conclusion about the finished product. No lawyer-client review, license search, patent search, or comprehensive trademark clearance was performed.

## Planning answer

The lower-risk path is to publish an independently expressed game built around the abstract functional loop:

> a counterfactual premise + a target + a deduction or consequence to evaluate

That conclusion is a **planning inference**, not a holding about this exact game. No located court opinion decides this precise structure. The inference follows from 17 U.S.C. § 102(b), Copyright Office guidance, *Baker v. Selden*, and game cases treating rules, winning conditions, roles, and mechanics as systems or methods rather than copyrightable expression.[C1–C6]

The boundary is not permission to reproduce the scene that supplied the inspiration. Every player-facing word, example, fictional detail, label, illustration, screen, sound, animation, and branding choice should be independently created. *Castle Rock* is a direct warning: a trivia product copied protectable fictional expression from a television series even when many questions described invented events rather than quoting dialogue.[C7]

For product planning:

1. **Keep the mechanic; replace the expression.** Implement the functional loop with original rules text, terminology, examples, data, scoring, interface, art, audio, and presentation.
2. **Do not ship source-show content.** Exclude dialogue, close paraphrases, characters, fictional incidents and relationships, stills, clips, voices, music, set or costume imagery, and signature props unless counsel clears or licenses them.
3. **Do not market through the source.** Keep the program, episode, character, actor, studio, and catchphrases out of the title, subtitle, logo, store metadata, search keywords, screenshots, trailer, and launch copy unless counsel approves the exact use.
4. **Treat the working title as provisional.** A limited USPTO database check found no record for the exact phrase **ONE FACT CHANGED**, but that is not trademark clearance.
5. **Obtain counsel review before launch.** The gate should cover the identified source scene, final content inventory, title/logo clearance, marketing, likeness/voice issues, and every target country.

## Element-by-element boundary

| Product element | Planning position | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract premise → target → deduction structure | May be implemented independently | A functional rule structure is likely an idea, system, process, or method under § 102(b); the exact structure has not been adjudicated.[C1–C6] |
| Turn order, validation logic, scoring, win conditions, and role functions | Design independently and document their functional purpose | Game rules and methods are generally outside copyright, but copied prose or expressive presentation is not.[C2–C6] |
| Rules text, onboarding, prompts, help, and error copy | Write from scratch | Copyright can protect original wording even when the underlying method is free.[C2–C5] |
| Generic functional vocabulary such as “premise,” “target,” and “deduction” | Usable as ordinary terminology, subject to title/mark clearance | Names, titles, and short phrases usually lack standalone copyright protection, but source-identifying use can create trademark risk.[C3][T1–T3] |
| A source-specific coined term, character name, catchphrase, or distinctive phrase | Avoid absent counsel clearance | Short phrases may fall outside copyright in isolation yet still signal source, reproduce character expression in context, or function as marks.[C3][T1–T5] |
| Player-authored counterfactuals | Prefer over a source-derived prompt library | It reduces embedded copying, though moderation and user-submission procedures are separate issues outside this memo. |
| Original fictional examples | Use after source-independence review | Examples can teach the system without importing the program’s characters, plots, or dialogue. |
| Quotes, close paraphrases, or recreated fictional incidents from the scene | Do not include without license or specific counsel advice | *Castle Rock* rejected the premise that fictional “facts” were free facts and found entertainment-oriented repackaging not fair use.[C7][C8] |
| Show stills, clips, music, voices, set/costume likenesses, or signature props | Do not include without clearance | These can implicate copyright, trademark/trade dress, and publicity or passing-off rules independently of the mechanic.[C6][T1–T7] |
| An original visual and sonic identity | Required | The freedom to reuse mechanics does not extend to protectable graphics, audiovisual expression, or nonfunctional source-identifying design.[C6][T1][T6][T7] |
| Show or character name in title, subtitle, logo, store listing, SEO, or ads | Avoid unless counsel approves the exact presentation | Such use can suggest affiliation, sponsorship, approval, or origin, and may itself function as branding.[T1–T5] |
| A factual statement naming the inspiration | Counsel gate, not a default launch tactic | Nominative-use rules vary by circuit and turn on necessity, amount used, and whether the presentation suggests sponsorship.[T4][T5] |
| Disclaimer | Use only as one risk-reduction fact, never as permission | A disclaimer does not cure copied expression or guarantee the absence of likely confusion; *Jack Daniel’s* involved a disclaimed product and still required ordinary trademark analysis.[T4] |

## Copyright analysis

### Mechanics and expression

17 U.S.C. § 102(b) excludes ideas, procedures, processes, systems, methods of operation, concepts, principles, and discoveries from copyright regardless of the form in which they are described.[C1] Copyright Office guidance applies that line directly to games: a game’s idea, title, and methods of play are not protected by copyright, while original rules text and graphic or literary material may be.[C2][C3]

*Baker v. Selden* remains the foundational distinction: copyright in a work explaining a system does not confer an exclusive right to practice the system.[C4] That is a copyright rule, not a patent freedom-to-operate opinion.

Two modern game decisions illustrate both sides:

- In *DaVinci Editrice v. ZiKo Games*, substantially similar rules, roles, winning conditions, and character abilities were treated as unprotectable gameplay methods; different theme, names, art, and expression mattered.[C5] This is a persuasive federal district-court decision, not a nationwide holding about all games.
- In *Tetris Holding v. Xio Interactive*, the court separated unprotectable game rules from protectable labels, graphics, board and piece presentation, and audiovisual expression. Copying a game’s nearly identical expressive look was actionable even though the rules were free to implement.[C6] This too is a fact-specific district-court decision.

Applied here, the abstract input and evaluation roles in “premise + target + deduction” are likely functional. A particular script explaining them, a curated expressive sequence of prompts, a show-derived fictional database, or a distinctive presentation is not made free merely because it serves the mechanic.

### Television dialogue, examples, and paraphrases

There is no safe number of words that may be copied. Fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107 weighs purpose and character, nature of the work, amount and substantiality, and market effect; it is a case-specific defense, not a content budget.[C8]

*Castle Rock Entertainment v. Carol Publishing* is particularly relevant. A commercial *Seinfeld* trivia book drew questions and answers from the series. The Second Circuit held that invented program events were protectable fictional expression—not unprotected real-world facts—and rejected fair use where the new work repackaged the series for entertainment rather than commenting on it.[C7] Some questions quoted dialogue, but avoiding quotation alone did not resolve the copying.

Accordingly:

- do not convert the source scene into example cards, trivia, canonical answers, character relationships, or deductions;
- do not assume that paraphrasing dialogue or plot details avoids infringement;
- do not rely on a free beta, small excerpt, attribution, “fan-made” label, homage, or disclaimer as a fair-use rule;
- if commentary on the source is genuinely part of a proposed product, have counsel review the exact content and context before relying on fair use.

The simplest launch corpus is source-independent: player-authored inputs plus original examples set in new situations with no recognizable characters, fictional history, dialogue, or scene composition.

## Title, terminology, marketing, and trademark

### Confusion and source-identifying use

The Lanham Act can reach uses likely to cause confusion about affiliation, connection, association, origin, sponsorship, or approval, including unregistered marks and protectable trade dress.[T1–T3] Copyright availability does not answer that separate question.

*Jack Daniel’s Properties v. VIP Products* holds that when an accused designation is used as a mark for the defendant’s own goods, ordinary likelihood-of-confusion analysis applies; expressive or humorous content does not automatically trigger a special threshold test.[T4] The Court did not decide whether the *Rogers* test is appropriate for uses that do not designate source, and it did not finally decide whether the product before it was confusing. Product presentation therefore matters.

The Ninth Circuit’s nominative-use framework in *New Kids on the Block* asks whether the product can readily be identified without the mark, whether only reasonably necessary use was made, and whether anything suggests sponsorship or endorsement.[T5] That test is circuit-specific and fact-dependent. It should inform risk reduction, not be treated as a nationwide safe harbor.

The operational rule is to sell this game under its own identity. Do not use the source program as the acquisition channel without review: no “the [show] game,” character-forward subtitle, studio-like logo, source catchphrase, show name in store keywords, scene still in a screenshot, or ad copy implying an official adaptation.

### Preliminary working-title check

On 2026-09-09, two limited searches in the USPTO Trademark Search system—an exact combined-mark search for **ONE FACT CHANGED** and an ordered wildcard search for the same words—returned no record. This establishes only that those narrow queries returned no federal record at that time.

It does **not** clear the title. USPTO guidance says a proper search must look for confusingly similar marks used with related goods and services, not just exact matches; federal results are not a legal conclusion, and even dead federal records may coexist with common-law rights.[T8][T9] A comprehensive search may include federal and state records, business names, domains, app and game marketplaces, the internet, and relevant foreign databases.[T9]

Registration and freedom to use are also different questions. The USPTO generally refuses registration of the title of a single creative work while permitting marks for a series when the evidence supports source-identifying use.[T10] Counsel should clear the final wording, spelling and sound variants, logo, goods/services, and target territories before the name becomes expensive to change.

## Visual identity and trade dress

The product should not evoke the source through a cumulative package of copied choices. Use an original:

- color system and typography;
- grid, card, board, and screen layout;
- iconography, illustration, animation, and transitions;
- logo, packaging, store assets, and promotional composition;
- sound design, voices, music, and interaction feedback.

*Tetris* shows that independently writable mechanics do not permit replication of a game’s expressive audiovisual presentation.[C6] Trademark law adds a separate constraint: product design cannot be inherently distinctive under *Wal-Mart v. Samara Brothers* and must acquire distinctiveness to be protected, while functional features cannot receive trade-dress protection under *TrafFix Devices v. Marketing Displays*.[T6][T7] Those limits do not make a copied overall look safe; nonfunctional, source-identifying presentation can still support a claim, and copyright may protect individual artwork or audiovisual choices.

A different palette alone is not enough if typography, composition, props, character silhouettes, set geometry, sound cues, and promotional framing still point to the program.

## Jurisdiction limits

There is no single worldwide copyright or trademark clearance.

- The Berne Convention requires national treatment and independence of protection, while the scope and remedies are generally governed by the law of the country where protection is claimed.[J1]
- TRIPS carries an international idea/expression baseline for copyright, but national causes of action, exceptions, neighboring rights, trademark rules, and remedies still differ.[J2] Copyright Office Circular 38A likewise explains the treaty-based, country-specific structure of international copyright relations.[J3]
- In the United States, *Abitron v. Hetronic* holds that Lanham Act §§ 1114(1)(a) and 1125(a)(1) are not extraterritorial and extend only to domestic infringing “use in commerce.”[J4] The opinion does not turn a global online launch into a safe harbor; which online conduct is domestic and what foreign law applies remain fact-specific.
- The EU Information Society Directive allows member states to implement specified quotation and parody, caricature, or pastiche exceptions subject to stated conditions and the three-step test.[J5] This is not the U.S. open-ended fair-use rule, and implementation varies.
- U.K. law separately recognizes fair dealing for caricature, parody, or pastiche.[J6] In *Nova Productions v. Mazooma Games*, commonplace game ideas and mechanics did not prevent an independently coded and graphically different game.[J7] By contrast, *Shazam Productions v. Only Fools The Dining Experience* found infringement where an interactive tribute reused developed television characters, their mannerisms, backstories, catchphrases, and script material; its parody/pastiche defense failed on the facts.[J8]

Choose launch countries before final clearance. A U.S.-only legal assessment cannot support a worldwide app-store release, and geofencing or a jurisdiction clause is not by itself a determination that foreign rights are irrelevant.

## Counsel gates before publication

Qualified counsel should review the product before public launch if any of the following remains:

1. **Source identification:** the exact program, episode, scene, script, rights holders, and any licensed elements are known and can be compared against the final build.
2. **Content inventory:** any quote, paraphrase, character analogue, fictional event, relationship, still, clip, voice, music, prop, costume, set reference, or source-derived example remains.
3. **Brand clearance:** the final title, subtitle, logo, slogan, domain, app-store name, social handles, and related-goods/services search are complete in each launch territory.
4. **Marketing review:** store metadata, SEO terms, screenshots, trailer, press copy, influencer brief, and any statement identifying the inspiration are final.
5. **Visual comparison:** the complete interface, motion, audio, packaging, and campaign are compared against both the source program and competing games.
6. **People depicted or imitated:** actor or character name, likeness, voice, performance, endorsement implication, or local personality/publicity right is involved.
7. **Territory plan:** counsel can apply the law of every country where the service is targeted, sold, promoted, or materially used.
8. **Other rights:** patent freedom to operate, contracts, platform rules, user-generated-content procedures, privacy, and moderation need separate assessment if applicable.

If source recognition is commercially necessary, the decision is no longer merely “independent mechanics.” Ask counsel whether a license, narrower factual reference, or different campaign is required before publishing the reference.
